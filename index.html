The sequence

One feed, two units, no choices in between: the flash splits the feed into vapour V and liquid L, and L — the whole of it, at whatever flow and composition the flash gives — is the stripper feed. That is why the sidebar has no stripper-feed inputs.

F = V + L   ·   F·zi = V·yi + L·xi

The flash

At the flash temperature and pressure, each component splits between the phases according to its K-value. Raoult's law gets K from the pure-component vapour pressure, which the Antoine equation supplies:

Ki = Psati(T) / P   ·   log10 Psat = A − B / (T + C)   ·   yi = Ki·xi

The one unknown is β = V/F, the vapour fraction. Requiring both phase compositions to sum to 1 gives the Rachford–Rice equation, which the solver bisects:

Σi zi(Ki − 1) / (1 + β(Ki − 1)) = 0

Two checks come first: if Σ ziKi ≤ 1 the feed is all liquid (β = 0, its bubble point is above P); if Σ zi/Ki ≤ 1 it is all vapour (β = 1). With β known, xi = zi / (1 + β(Ki − 1)) and yi = Kixi.

Default case on the App tab: Psat at 25 °C is 9.53 bar for propane and 0.0318 bar for water, so K = 9.53 and 0.0318 at 1 bar — propane overwhelmingly prefers the vapour, water the liquid, and β comes out 0.2278.

The stripper

The column is isothermal with constant molar overflow, and only propane is tracked transferring. On every stage the leaving streams are in equilibrium, and a balance on the stage ties them to the streams entering:

yn = K·xn   ·   L·xn−1 + G·yn+1 = L·xn + G·yn

The gas enters pure (yN+1 = 0). The bottom outlet xN is the one unknown: the solver guesses it, steps up the column with those two equations, and adjusts the guess until the top liquid it computes equals the flash liquid. On the stepping diagram this is the staircase between the equilibrium line (slope K) and the operating line (slope L/G, through (xout, 0) and (xin, ytop)).

Everything about how well a stripper can do is in one number, the stripping factor. The Kremser equation then gives the result for pure inlet gas in closed form — the solver uses it only as a check on its own stepping, and warns if the two disagree:

S = K·G / L   ·   xout/xin = (S − 1) / (SN+1 − 1)    (= 1/(N + 1) when S = 1)

Why S ≤ 1 is a wall, not a slope: if S < 1, then as N → ∞ the fraction remaining tends to (1 − S), not to 0. At S = 0.247 (the App tab with the gas cut to 2 mol/h) at least 75.3% of the propane stays in the water no matter how many stages are added — and the computed outlet, 76,814 ppm against an inlet of 101,956 ppm, is 75.3% of it. The fix is never more stages; it is more gas, or a higher K (raise T, lower P).

Assumptions and limits

This is a teaching model. Propane and water are nearly immiscible — real propane solubility in water is far below what Raoult's law predicts here, because the activity coefficients are very large. A predictive model needs activity coefficients or an equation of state with a 3-phase flash. Also assumed: one liquid phase (no separate liquid propane — the solver warns near propane's critical temperature, 96.7 °C), an isothermal column, constant L and G, inert gas that does not dissolve, and no water carried into the stripper gas. Antoine constants are NIST fits with limited temperature ranges, and the solver warns outside them.

This is solver.py itself — the same file the App tab runs — loaded live and split at its section markers, so the code here can never drift out of date with the code doing the calculations.
